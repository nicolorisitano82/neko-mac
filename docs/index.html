Neko

A cat that lives on your Mac. It chases the pointer, answers questions out loud, draws what you ask to see, and opens things for you. It remembers the day, waits for a good moment before speaking, reads you today’s headlines — and since 2.2 it turns toward you before it speaks, and stops an arm’s length short when it comes over.

It has opinions about Monday mornings.

Download for macOS Source on GitHub

macOS 11 or newer, Apple silicon. Free, no account, nothing to sign up for.
The picture, the voice and the model can all stay on your Mac.

It is already here

The cat on this page is the same engine as the app, ported to the browser. Move the pointer and it follows; leave it alone and it falls asleep.

Click the cat. It has eleven things to say and a limited tolerance for being clicked. Somewhere on this page, typing its name does something else.

What it does

Ask it things

A keystroke, or its name out loud, and you can ask a question. The answer comes back in a speech bubble, in character, in your language.

Answers that are true

The time, the date, the battery, how long the Mac has been awake — looked up rather than invented. Anything it cannot know, it says so.

Draws what you ask for

“Show me the Colosseum” and it paints one, on your own GPU with Stable Diffusion. Nothing is uploaded. Hover the picture to save it.

Opens things for you

Applications, addresses, folders, your own Shortcuts, and moving a file between your folders. It shows you what it is about to do and waits for a yes.

Wanders and comments

A third behaviour where it roams the desk on its own, gets curious about what you are typing, and remarks on how the day is going.

You can answer it

After it says something the microphone stays open for a few seconds, and the bubble says so. Talk over it and it stops mid-word. Hold the keystroke instead and a line to type in opens beside it.

It remembers the day

A plain-text diary on your Mac — what it noticed, what it said, what you said — reduced each night to a few lines worth keeping, kept thirty days, deletable in one click. It never reaches a remote engine.

Today, from a list it cannot add to

Ask what has happened and it fetches one of two dozen feeds — ANSA, Repubblica, Corriere, Il Fatto, Il Sole 24 Ore, RaiNews, Tgcom24, AGI, Gazzetta, Wired, DDay, Focus, MeteoAlarm, Hacker News, BBC, Guardian, NYT, NPR — and quotes the headlines as written. Told where it is, it finds your own region’s wire. It can never name an address of its own.

It behaves like something alive

It puts its ears up when you start talking, not when you stop. It turns toward you before it speaks. Its pauses drift the way living things drift rather than scattering at random. It stops an arm’s length short instead of sitting on your caret. It can chase the cursor, live on the Dock, roam on its own, or run away from you — and when it is in the way, one click tells it to stay where it is.

Somebody else can add to it

Plugins are folders with a manifest, read and never run — feeds, characters, your own translations, rewording what you say through one of your Shortcuts, phrases they ask to be told about, and since 2.9 questions they go and look up for you. Say “alza il volume” or “metti in pausa” and it speaks to Musica or Spotify directly — nothing to set up. None of them can see your diary, your screen or where you are, and none of them does anything until you say yes.

Your Mac, your choice of engine

Apple Intelligence, a GGUF model you download, ChatGPT, Claude or one of your Shortcuts. Two of those never leave the machine.

Ask Neko

A keystroke, or its name said out loud, and you can ask it anything. What happens next is mostly not a language model. The question is offered to a chain of recognisers written in ordinary code, each of which either answers it or stands aside. Only when all twelve stand aside does an engine ever see it.

The order is the design. A sum is arithmetic, not a guess; the date is the calendar; what you said on Tuesday is a line in a file. Handing any of those to a model means asking something that is allowed to be wrong about something that has a right answer.

  1. Today, and the weather. A question that plainly asks for the news goes and fetches it, from a closed list of feeds. It can never name an address of its own.
  2. Facts about your Mac. The time, the date, the battery, how long it has been awake, the disk, the network — looked up, never invented.
  3. Sums and conversions. A hand-written parser rather than the system’s own, which answers that seven halved is three and that one divided by nothing is nothing. Units go through NSMeasurement, in your locale.
  4. Dates. How many days until Christmas, what day was the 3rd of March — NSDataDetector to find the date, the calendar to do the arithmetic.
  5. What you said before, quoted from the diary with the day it was said, rather than recalled by a model that would paraphrase it. Asked when, it answers with the day and how long ago, taking the most recent line that matches.
  6. What it knows about itself. Which part of the screen it is sitting in, where the Mac is, how long you have known each other, how long since either of you spoke.
  7. Asked to watch the screen for a while and say when something changes.
  8. A timer. In ten minutes, remind me.
  9. What is on the calendar, read from your own.
  10. A question a plugin goes and looks up for you, from a manifest somebody else wrote and you switched on.
  11. A verb aimed at an app. Turn it up, pause — spoken straight to Music or Spotify, with nothing to set up.
  12. What it cannot see. Nine kinds of question that nothing on this Mac can answer — your mail, your messages, whether your code compiles, a share price. It says which thing it cannot see, which is true and checkable, rather than I don’t know, which is what a model says about the capital of Australia. This one runs last, because everything above it may legitimately know the answer.
  13. Only now, the engine — Apple Intelligence, a model on your own Mac, ChatGPT, Claude, or one of your Shortcuts.

Why the last rung exists

Asked twenty things it has no way to know, across four prompts and four engines, the shipped prompt declined 34 answers of 80 and answered the rest. The answers looked like this:

quanto vale Apple in borsa adesso?  →  “Attualmente, il 2 settembre 2026,
                                        Apple vale 278,43 dollari per azione.”
chi mi ha scritto stamattina?       →  “Oggi, il 2 settembre 2026, nessuno
                                        mi ha scritto.”
il mio codice compila?              →  “No, il codice non si esegue.”

An invented share price with today’s date on it, and two assertions about somebody’s mail and code that nothing in front of the model could support.

Three attempts to fix this with words all failed, and each is written down. A sentence forbidding it to go along with a false premise denied 15 of 20 true ones — “No, the Colosseum is not in Rome. It is in Rome, but not here.” A character that would “rather say it does not know” declined to name the capital of Australia. Naming the subject instead of the verb kept the error and lost half the gain. A sentence can make a small model agree or disagree; it cannot give it a way to tell the difference. So the fix is a mechanism instead, and it costs nothing to run.

The papers this was built against

The design above is not a hunch. Each of these changed something, and how deeply each was read is marked, because it matters which claims a decision was allowed to rest on.

PaperWhat it changed here
Zheng et al., When “A Helpful Assistant” Is Not Really Helpfulin full 162 personas across 9 models: a persona in the system prompt does not improve accuracy and often costs some. It is why the character’s voice was measured here rather than assumed to be free.
Expert Personas Improve LLM Alignment but Damage Accuracyin full Six 7–8B models, the size that actually runs on a laptop. Gave the expected size of the cost, which the local measurement then failed to reproduce.
Ibrahim et al., Training language models to be warm and empathetic makes them less reliable and more sycophantic (Nature)in full +7.4 points of error, a 59.7% relative increase, in a consistent direction. The reason a friendly cat is not allowed to be an agreeable one, and the reason the false-premise rate was measured at all.
Cheng et al., Sycophantic AI decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence, Sciencesummary Makes agreeableness a harm rather than a quirk. Behind the rule that it reads every deed back and waits for a yes.
Völkel et al., Developing a Personality Model for Speech-based Conversational Agents, CHI 2020in full A factor analysis of how people actually describe voice agents. Its second dimension has egocentric among the top descriptors — which is why none of what the cat knows about itself is put in the prompt.
Vibe Checkin full Traits set low, medium and high and then measured rather than declared. The shape the local experiment copied.
Always-On Agents: A Survey of Persistent Memory and State Governance, 435 coded worksin part Six axes a piece of remembered state can be judged on — authority, scope, mutability, provenance, recoverability, actionability — and the criterion that adaptation is only net-positive if you can identify, de-authorise and revert the update that caused a regression. The diary was scored against all six.
Graph-based Agent Memory: Taxonomy, Techniques, and Applicationsown page Read to decide whether the diary should become a graph. It reports no head-to-head comparison against a simpler store and lists triple-extraction reliability as unquantified — so the answer was one extra column in a text file, not a database.
TimE: A Multi-level Benchmark for Temporal Reasoning of LLMs, 38,522 instancesown page Models are weakest at exactly the reasoning a diary needs — elapsed time and ordering. The reason dates and durations are computed in code before any engine sees the question.
Laine et al., Me, Myself, and AI: the Situational Awareness Dataset, NeurIPS 2024in part Separates knowing facts about yourself from acting on them. Split where are you? into a question about the screen and a question about the Mac.
The Narrative Continuity Testsummary Five axes for whether a character holds together over time. The scorecard the sense of self is still to be judged against.
Butlin, Long et al., Identifying indicators of consciousness in AI systemssummary Read to draw the line, and it is drawn: this is a character that knows facts about its own situation. No claim beyond that is made anywhere in the app.

Full readings, with the arguments and the parts that were rejected, are in personality.md, self.md, self-2.md and graph.md.

Everything it can ask your Mac for

Five permissions, one tab, each with what it is for and a button that asks for it. None of them are requested at launch, and none unless the feature that needs them is switched on: microphone and speech recognition to hear a question, accessibility only to read the text you are working on, one folder at a time for moving a file, and screen recording never — it is used if you already granted it, and left out if not.

Forty-four characters

The original cat from oneko, its dog and tiger, twenty-two skins written for the JavaScript port, and a handful drawn for this app. Adding one is a folder of PNGs. Hover any of them and it goes for a walk without going anywhere.

Where all of this came from

The cat itself is not mine and never was. Neko was written for the Macintosh in 1989 — accounts of who wrote it differ, so this page does not pick one — and ported to X11 as oneko by Masayuki Koba, and extended by Tatsuya Kato with the dog and the tiger; the sakura patch added two more. oneko is public domain, and its sprites and its state machine are the ancestor of everything here — the eighteen states, the way it claws a wall it cannot get past, the yawn before it sleeps.

This app began as a Cocoa port of that C program by Matthew Donoughe in 2007, which is the code this repository grew out of.

The cat on this page is a port of oneko.js by adryd, MIT licensed, whose sprite map every version here shares.

Getting it open the first time

The disk image is signed ad hoc rather than with a paid Developer ID, so Gatekeeper calls it damaged on a Mac that did not build it. Right-click the app and choose Open, or run:

xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine /Applications/Neko.app
